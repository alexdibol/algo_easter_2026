Algorithmic Trading Systems under Governance

This landing page provides a single, orderly point of access for the Easter course at Cambridge Judge Business School. The course presents algorithmic trading as a full system of judgment, design, implementation, testing, execution, documentation, auditability, and institutional governance. Its purpose is not simply to show students a catalogue of trading strategies, but to help them understand how a trading idea becomes a responsible algorithmic system.

The books and decks linked below are therefore not isolated files. They operate as structured gateways into a wider learning architecture. Chapter by chapter, students can move from written explanations to videos, podcasts, specialized decks, and Google Colab notebooks. The design is deliberately multimodal: students can prepare before class, revisit difficult ideas after class, and explore implementation resources at a pace that matches their curiosity, technical background, and learning objectives.

Course Orientation

The course is built as a guided path from intuition to institutional discipline. Students begin with market ideas and strategy families, but gradually learn to ask the deeper questions that define professional algorithmic trading: What evidence supports the signal? What risks are hidden in the data? What happens under stress? Who is responsible for the model? How is the system documented, reviewed, improved, and governed?

Purpose: Help students transform trading intuition into transparent, testable, and governable algorithmic systems.
Pedagogy: Combine lectures, books, decks, audiovisual resources, notebooks, and structured experimentation so that concepts can be learned, revisited, implemented, and challenged.
Method: Move repeatedly between idea, code, evidence, execution, risk, audit trail, and human responsibility.
Course Framework

From trading ideas to governed algorithmic systems

The Easter course treats algorithmic trading as a complete institutional workflow and as a progressive learning journey. Students first encounter the conceptual language of algorithmic systems, then use playground materials to explore strategy ideas, then study how AI agents can support research and implementation, and finally enter the laboratory environment where systems are tested, documented, stress-tested, and governed. The pedagogical objective is to make students comfortable moving across four connected layers: conceptual understanding, creative exploration, technical implementation, and institutional accountability.

1

Conceptual Foundation

The course presentation introduces the intellectual map of the programme. It explains why algorithmic trading is a systems discipline rather than a purely technical coding exercise, how governance changes the way models are designed and evaluated, and why serious trading education must connect markets, data, signals, code, execution, risk, evidence, and institutional accountability.

2

Creative Playground

The playground materials give students a structured space for experimentation. They are designed to encourage curiosity without abandoning discipline: students can explore trading hypotheses, compare signal families, test portfolio ideas, and use AI responsibly as an assistant for explanation, critique, code generation, and iteration, while remaining accountable for the final logic.

3

Agents and Laboratory

The agents and lab resources move the course toward operational maturity. Students see how agent-supported workflows can assist with research, coding, diagnostics, risk review, reporting, and governance checks. The laboratory materials then translate those ideas into reproducible notebooks, systematic backtests, stress tests, audit artifacts, and responsible human oversight.

Course Evaluation

Assessment as a governed trading-system exercise

The evaluation of the course is designed to test more than technical familiarity with trading ideas. Students are expected to demonstrate that they can think like designers of governed algorithmic systems: defining a trading problem, explaining the logic of the strategy, implementing or specifying the workflow, evaluating evidence, identifying risks, documenting assumptions, and defending the final result with professional clarity. The assignment should therefore be approached as a complete system-design exercise rather than as a narrow backtesting task.

1

Design the system, not only the strategy

Students should show how the trading idea becomes a structured algorithmic system. This includes the investment hypothesis, the data used, the signal logic, the universe selection, the portfolio or execution logic, and the governance controls that make the system reviewable rather than merely computational.

2

Evaluate evidence and risk

The work should explain what the backtest or analytical evidence suggests, but also what it does not prove. Students should discuss performance indicators, stress testing, robustness, transaction costs, slippage, regime sensitivity, model limitations, and the danger of overfitting or retrospective storytelling.

3

Document AI use responsibly

The use of AI is not discouraged. However, students remain responsible for the final submission. Any use of AI should be disclosed, explained, and controlled: what it helped with, how the student reviewed it, where human judgment intervened, and why the final content remains the student's own defended work.

Read the evaluation instructions carefully

The evaluation document provides the formal instructions for the course assessment. Students should consult it before choosing a topic, designing the system, using AI support, preparing the essay or report, and finalizing the evidence package. The central expectation is that the submitted work should be clear, rigorous, reproducible where relevant, and explicit about assumptions, limitations, governance, and human responsibility.
